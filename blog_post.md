Using AI to predict the Oscar's in easy. Building the infrastructure that lets anyone generate predictive models (human or agent) is harder.
I wanted to create that data foundation. Not to accurately predict the Oscars (though that would be cool) but to show how a solid data infrastructure enables one to generate predictive models relatively easily, and how this same architecture can be applied to any domain. This infrastructure can serve as a blue print for other domains that may be, dare I say, more impactful than the Oscars 😲.
The idea is simple: build a data foundation such that anyone can, with an agent, say "build me a model to predict the Oscars. I think x, y, and z, will be important features in making predictions." This data foundation is a knowledge graph. What is a knowledge graph? Here's an article I wrote recently that goes deep on that question but the short answer is, "A knowledge graph is a layered knowledge system in which ontologies define meaning, controlled vocabularies catalog entities, and observational data provides evidence - allowing knowledge to accumulate, evolve, and be reasoned over as understanding improves."

Overall architecture
The image below shows the overall architecture. On the bottom level we have the foundation. That is the core ontology as well as the instance data. Together, this creates a core knowledge graph that can be expanded as needed for building different predictive models. The layer above that is inference - once we have the ontology and instance data together, we can infer a lot of additional data. The enrichment step is on top of that. Each film in the data foundation has multiple IDs associated with it: The Movie Database (TMDB); IMDb; and Wikidata. Enrichment is where the user can use the IDs associated with each film and the corresponding APIs to bring in additional data, depending on what kind of model they want to build and what they deem important. Finally, we take the enriched graph to build a predictive model and record our forecasts.
🧠 Layer one: the data foundation
The foundation is both the ontology and the instance data. In previous posts, I went through how the medical field has used knowledge graphs to manage complex information and advance clinical research and patient care. A key reason this has worked is the clear distinction between an ontology (the theory of what exists and how those things interact with each other) and the controlled vocabularies that populate those categories with real-world entities. Here is an example of what that looks like in medicine:
In medicine, the ontology defines things like drugs, pathogens, and diseases. For the Oscars, our ontology needs to define things like movies, nominees, and nominations. 
For the Oscars, this is what the ontology looks like.
The goal of this knowledge graph is to create models to predict who will win an Academy Award in any given category. The key class in the ontology, therefore, is nomination. For any category, there is a set of nominations, and we are going to try to predict which of those nominations results in a win. A nomination is associated with both a film, and, usually, at least one person. The person, or group of people associated with the nomination are the nominees. 
A nomination also has an award ceremony: the "98th Academy Awards" for example, and an award system: Academy Awards, BAFTAs, Golden Globes, etc. The 98th Academy Awards ceremony belongs to the Academy Awards award system. Each nomination also has a category: best editing, best director, etc. Since different award systems use different categories, for the purposes of this knowledge graph, I have created the class Award Concept to enable cross-system alignment. For example, the Academy Awards have a category called "Best Actor in a Leading Role" while the SAG awards have "Actor Award for Outstanding Performance by a Male Actor in a Leading Role." These are, in this ontology, both realizations of the same award concept. I learned that this is crucial because one of the best predictors of whether a nomination results in a win are precursor award ceremonies. The Oscars tend to be the last award ceremony so we want to ensure we are taking into account who won at the Golden Globes, the BAFTAs, the SAG awards, the Producers Guild of America (PGA) Awards and the Directors Guild of America (DGA) awards. 
Since we are using this to create predictions, we also want to store those predictions in our graph, along with others we can find to calibrate. For any nomination we can have many forecasts, which we organize into forecast sets. For example, we ran our model on February 21st to predict the Best Actress category of the BAFTAs, which generated a forecast set. That forecast set contains information on the model and features used, and the date. It also contains individual forecasts for each nominee: Emma Stone, Jessie Buckley, Kate Hudson, Renate Reinsve, and Rose Byrne. 
Each film also has idenfiers associated with it. For this graph, so far, I have incorporated IMDb ids, TMDB ids, and Wikidata ids. These are a crucial piece of metadata because they are the way this data foundation can become a much larger graph that can be used to generate predictive models. This schema defines properties for revenue, budget, and ratings, but there are no values for them. But using these ids, and information about what the ids can be used for (accessing APIs), an agent can retrieve that data. 
Instance data
The ontology defines the classes, relationships, properties, and key constraints. The instance data is how we populate the ontology to create a graph. The key piece of instance data are the Oscar nominations themselves. I found this awesome dataset from David Lu which has many Oscar nominations going back to 1927. It was a csv so I converted to RDF and aligned it with our ontology. 
What does it mean that the instance data is aligned with our ontology? Here is what the instance of the nomination for the movie Sinners for best. picture actually looks like in RDF format:
The top line says that "Nomination_oscars_2026_best_picture_sinners_c477fbb973cc84ff" is an instance of Nomination. That is its unique ID, or Universal Resource Identifier (URI). It follows the structure: type (nomination)_award system (oscars)_year (2026)_category (best picture)_film name (sinners).
The reason there is a bunch of random letters and numbers at the end, by the way, is just in case there are two movies with the same name nominated for the same category in the same year at the same award system. Unlikely, but possible. Remember the year everyone made a Pinocchio movie?
The rest of the rows are properties of this nomination: the label is "Oscars - 2026 - BEST PICTURE - Sinners", the category, the ceremony, etc. Each of these rows is what is called a triple i.e. a way of structuring data using three parts: subject, predicate, object. The nomination (subject) has category (predicate) best picture (object). The nomination (subject) has nominee (predicate) Ryan Coogler (object). Ryan Coogler is an instance of a person who has been nominated for other categories for other movies, and that information is all easily retrievable, as long as we use the same URI for Ryan Coogler (i.e. Person_Ryan_Coogler). 
What's cool about storing data this way is that we basically just have a long list of nominations, a long list of people, and a long list of movies, but as long as we have an ontology to align them and URIs defined clearly they become an interconnected graph without any additional effort. 
The data foundation has a total of 366,784 triples and is about 17MB in size. 
🔁 Layer two: inferences
When we put the instance data and the ontology together, for example, we can find inferences - additional triples that were not explicitly listed in the data. For example, here is how Ryan Coogler appears in the instance data:
There is no information about his movies or nominations. Here is how Sinners appears in the data:
There is no information about what people were involved in making the movie or what it was nominated for. But the nomination told us that Sinners was nominated for best picture at the 2026 Oscars. And in our ontology we have defined an inverse property "hasNomination" as the inverse of has nominee. This means that the data explicitly tells us that the "2026 Best Picture Sinners" nomination has nominee Ryan Coogler, and the ontology tells us that the relationship has nominee is an inverse of has nomination, therefore, we can infer that the person Ryan Coogler has nomination "2026 Best Picture Sinners". We can also infer that he was nominated for the movie Sinners. 
Now THAT is what I call a data foundation. We have an ontology and have populated it with instances of those things: nominations, films, people, awards, categories, etc. These lists of instances, by the way, go by many names: glossaries, taxonomies, reference data, thesaurus probably - I generally just use the generic term "controlled vocabularies". I know a lot of people in this space have a lot of opinions about this but I don't really think it matters 😬. What I have found matters is keeping the ontology (the classes and the relationships between them) separate from the instances (the lists of instances of the classes). 
The graph with inferred triples has grown to 522,503 triples, 23MB. 
🌍 Layer three: enrichment
The other crucial piece of this foundation is metadata: links to other datasets so we can enrich the graph. In this case, I have IMDb, TMDB, and Wikidata ids for every film. This allows any user to pull in whatever data they want. Be aware, this is how you can create a MASSIVE graph. For example, TMDB has info on every cast and crew member for every movie. That can be dozens of people. If you think that the key grip has predictive power, then pull that data in from the API. If you only want to predict best picture, then getting the educational background of every supporting actor from Wikidata is probably not important to you. 
Here is what I pulled in for my expanded graph:

🤖 Layer four: predictive models
Additional features calculated from the graph
In addition to all of the data we have stored directly in the graph, we can derive a bunch of other useful metrics like: previous nominations (sum, max, average), previous wins (sum, max), previous all nominations, previous all wins, precursor awards (PGA, DGA, SAG, BAFTA, Globes), acting nominations, directing nomination, writing nomination, editing nomination, cinematography nomination, above line nominations, technical nominations, music nominations, other nominations.
Feature selection

fckg/example_model/feature_selection.py
Now that we have a giant knowledge graph we can search for features that are good at predicting outcomes. I used a RFECV (recursive feature elimination with cross-validation) model to narrow down the features. I also included some logic to make this concept-aware. I mean we want to consider different conceptual groups of features (financial, ciritcal reception, popularity, etc.) but we also don't want two features that are too correlated with each other. For example, if we use the rotten tomatoes score then we don't also need the metacritic score. We also put a feature cap on the non-precursor features based on the number of training ceremonies available — roughly one feature per five ceremonies, following the events-per-variable (EPV) rule of thumb for logistic regression. Precursor award features (wins and nominations from BAFTA, Globes, SAG, DGA, PGA) are exempt from the cap because each represents a distinct expert signal.

My expanded graph gives me between 76 and 86 candidate features to choose from depending on the category. Film-level categories like Best Picture use 76; acting and directing categories get up to 86 because they include person-level features like prior nomination history for the individual nominee and cast pedigree metrics.

One thing worth noting: for acting categories, I added logic to group ceremony-level signals by category. For example, when predicting SAG Best Actress, both "BAFTA Best Actress winner" and "BAFTA Best Film winner" are signals that someone's film won at BAFTA — but they measure slightly different things. Rather than let the model see both and double-count the BAFTA signal, the feature selection step compares their predictive power head-to-head using univariate AUC and keeps only the stronger one. In practice for SAG Best Actress, the film-level BAFTA winner signal turned out to be empirically more predictive than the actress-specific one — a reminder that letting the data decide beats hardcoding assumptions.

Model selection

--- AI DRAFT - REWRITE IN YOUR VOICE ---

With features selected, the next step is figuring out which type of model actually performs best. I backtested four model types — Logistic Regression, Ridge Regression, Random Forest, and Gradient Boosting — against historical SAG ceremony data going back to 2016, measuring AUC, top-1 accuracy (did the model's #1 pick win?), and top-3 accuracy (was the winner in the model's top 3?).

For all five SAG categories, Ridge Regression won:

| Category | AUC | Top-1 | Top-3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Best Actress | 0.833 | 78% | 100% |
| Best Supporting Actress | 0.836 | 67% | 89% |
| Best Supporting Actor | 0.833 | 67% | 89% |
| Best Actor | 0.747 | 56% | 100% |
| Best Ensemble Cast | 0.735 | 56% | 100% |

The winner is almost always in the model's top 3. Best Actress was the strongest category — 78% top-1 accuracy over 9 backtested ceremonies.

Why Ridge and not Gradient Boosting, which is generally more powerful? With only ~31 SAG ceremonies in the training data, GB found spurious patterns. In an early run it ranked Jesse Plemons — who had zero precursor nominations — as the Best Actor favorite, entirely on the basis of a non-linear interaction with a previous-nominations variable. Ridge's L2 regularization prevents that kind of overfitting and keeps the model well-behaved when the training set is small.

Before the predictions, a quick note on validation.
